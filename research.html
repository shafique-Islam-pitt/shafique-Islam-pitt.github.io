Delivering Degrees: Educational Spillovers from Amazon's Logistics Expansion

Abstract. This paper estimates the causal effect of Amazon fulfillment center entry on local educational attainment by exploiting staggered facility openings across U.S. commuting zones between 2010 and 2023. Using a difference-in-differences framework, I find that Amazon's entry increases bachelor's degree attainment by 0.58 percentage points and master's degree attainment by 0.41 percentage points, with no evidence of the increases in high school dropout rates commonly associated with traditional resource booms. Amazon's expansion also raises four-year public college enrollment by 0.52 percentage points. The findings suggest two complementary mechanisms. First, higher household income relaxes financial constraints on educational investment. Second, Amazon's presence generates spillovers to information-intensive industries and high-skill occupations, increasing the returns to postsecondary education. The effects are concentrated among men and White students, indicating heterogeneous responses across demographic groups.

When Samples Shape Significance
With Sonia Ale, Lester Lusher, Huda Osman, and Jacob Stenstrom.

Abstract. Random sampling yields unbiased estimates, yet sampling variation alone can produce incorrect conclusions. Using a setting where repeated draws from the same data-generating process are observable, we replicate findings from 24 papers in top economics journals and re-estimate each result using 40 independent resamples. Across 3,640 resampled results, t-statistics shrink by 31%, frequently leading to lost significance. Proxies for researcher degrees of freedom and sampling noise predict reduced significance. Our exercise provides novel evidence quantifying the effect of sampling variability on empirical research and highlights how statistical evidence can vary even when holding research design fixed.
